How to read this map

What the satellite sees

The flood map comes from radar images taken by the Sentinel-1 satellite. Radar (a sensor that sends out radio waves and measures what bounces back) works through clouds and at night, which matters during the monsoon. Calm water reflects the waves away, so it looks dark. A computer model trained on labelled flood images marks those dark areas as water.

What "flooded" means here

A place counts as flooded when it had water during the flood but was usually dry in the same weeks of earlier years (not available). Rivers, ponds and wetlands that hold water every year are not counted. This also means that areas that flood every monsoon may be undercounted.

On the map, flood water is drawn in blue squares about not available across. A square is blue if any part of it was flooded, so thin channels stay visible. This makes the blue area look somewhat larger than the true flooded area.

What each village shape is

Each shape is the land within not available of a village point mapped in OpenStreetMap (a free map made by volunteers), and closer to that point than to any other village point. It is not an official village boundary. Only villages that someone has added to OpenStreetMap appear.

What the score means

The risk score runs from 0 to 100. It is a ranking of how exposed each village was to the flood and how much its people may rely on wells that floods can spoil. A higher score means the village should be looked at sooner.

The score is not a measurement of contamination. Nobody tested the water to make it. A village with a high score may have safe wells, and a village with a low score may not. Wells should be tested to confirm.

How a district team could use it

  1. Open the Ranked list and tick "Top 20 only".
  2. Print it or download the CSV, and check the names against your own village lists. Some names may differ from official spellings, and some villages are unnamed.
  3. Send water test kits and chlorine tablets to the top villages first, and test the hand pumps and shallow wells there.
  4. Use local knowledge to adjust. Field reports, ASHA worker reports or clinic data should come before this list when they disagree.
  5. Work down the list as supplies allow, and record test results so the ranking can be checked later.
